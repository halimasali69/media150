Artist Talk Reflection 4: Sara Stern

Sadia Ali, MEDPL 150

Photography and image making are how I slow down and notice the world. Most of my days are spent moving: on trains, through stations, between work, school, and home. A camera gives me a reason to stop for a second and ask what is actually happening around me. When I take a quick photo of myself on a subway platform, it is not only a selfie. It records where I was, what I wore, how tired or hopeful I looked, and what the space around me felt like at that time of day. Over many of these small images, a picture of my life and my city begins to form.

When I think about how I could represent our world at this moment, the first word that comes to mind is "in-between." So much of our lives right now happens in transit, on screens, or in spaces that belong to everyone and no one. Subway platforms, waiting rooms, and group chats are all places where we are present but also waiting for something else to begin. Photography is well suited to showing this feeling because a still image can hold a pause. A single frame of a person standing on a yellow safety line, headphones in, looking straight at the lens, can say a lot about modern attention, privacy, and the strange intimacy of crowded public life.

Illustration offers something different. A photograph shows what was in front of the lens, but an illustration can show what it felt like. If I redrew a crowded platform with exaggerated colors, repeating patterns, or figures that blur into one another, I could express anxiety, energy, or joy that the camera alone might not capture. I am interested in mixing the two: a real photograph with hand-drawn lines or flat color shapes layered on top. That combination reminds viewers that every image is both a record and an interpretation. It also fits our moment, when we constantly see edited, filtered, and remixed pictures and must decide what to trust.

Text adds another layer. A short caption, a line of overheard conversation, or a headline-like phrase placed next to an image can change its meaning completely. The same photo of an empty platform might feel peaceful with one title and lonely with another. I want to practice choosing words carefully so they open up an image instead of explaining it away. Pairing image and text is also a way of being honest about my point of view. It tells the viewer that someone made this, from a particular place, with particular experiences, rather than pretending the picture is a neutral window onto the world.

Listening to artists talk about their process reminds me that this kind of work does not require expensive equipment or perfect conditions. What matters is attention, patience, and a willingness to look again at the familiar. The artist's willingness to share personal experience through visual work encouraged me to trust my own perspective. My daily routes, my neighborhood, and the people I pass are all valid subjects. If I keep photographing, drawing, and writing about them, I can build a visual diary of this moment that is specific to me but also recognizable to others.

Going forward, I plan to use photography as a habit rather than an occasional assignment. I want to collect images over several weeks, look for patterns in what I choose to capture, and then use those patterns to shape a series. Whether it ends up as a set of portraits, a collage, or a small illustrated zine, my goal is the same: to show our world as it is felt, not only as it appears, and to make viewers feel a little more seen in the process.
